About

I am a PhD student in Chemical Engineering and Scientific Computing at the University of Michigan, advised by Prof. Bryan Goldsmith. My research uses generative machine learning and quantum chemistry to discover and design heterogeneous catalysts. I build models that generate catalyst structures with target properties, and I use density functional theory to understand how molecules behave at catalyst surfaces and electrochemical interfaces.

Before my PhD, I studied Chemical Engineering at West Virginia University, where I worked on modeling and optimizing hydrogen production processes. I also spent two and a half years as a process engineering co-op at Ingevity, where I developed real-time process monitoring tools for activated carbon production and coordinated training on them for over 3,000 plant personnel across multiple U.S. sites.

Preprints

arXiv:2605.141542026

TSAgent: An Agentic Workflow for Autonomous Transition State Search

V. Madhavan, A. Mathanker, D. M. Sweeney, O. A. Ohiro, Y. Wang, B. R. Goldsmith

Finding transition states is one of the most labor-intensive steps in computational catalysis and usually takes an expert's judgment to get calculations to converge. TSAgent is an autonomous workflow that locates transition states at DFT-level accuracy by repeatedly planning, running calculations, diagnosing failures from convergence and geometry feedback, and replanning. On held-out cases its success rate is comparable to that of expert human practitioners.

Publications

J. Am. Chem. Soc.2025Co-first author

Multilayer Formation, Interfacial Binding, and Stability of Self-Assembled Monolayers in Perovskite Solar Cells

C. A. Figueroa Morales‡, Z. Pizzo‡, D. M. Sweeney‡, Z. Hu‡, G. Sharma, S. Park, A. Dobre, S. Seong, A. T. Liu, N. Singh, B. R. Goldsmith, X. Gong (‡ equal contribution)

Self-assembled monolayers are increasingly used as the hole transport layer in perovskite solar cells, but how these molecules actually assemble and bind at the interface has been unclear. This experimental and computational collaboration shows that they can form multilayers rather than a single clean monolayer, and links the way they bind to the long-term stability of the device.

My contribution: I used density functional theory to determine the predominant formation and binding mechanisms of the monolayer molecules at the interface. These results were used to experimentally classify the hole transport layer and increase device stability 5-fold.

Communications Chemistry2025First author

A grand canonical study of the potential dependence of nitrate adsorption and dissociation across metals and dilute alloys

D. M. Sweeney, B. Tran, B. R. Goldsmith

Electrocatalytic nitrate reduction can turn a common water pollutant into useful products, but most computational studies treat the applied potential with a simple model. Using explicit and analytical grand canonical DFT across pure metals and Cu-based single-atom alloys, I showed that nitrate adsorption depends on potential more weakly than the standard computational hydrogen electrode assumes, and that the N–O bond-breaking barrier depends on it only slightly. Both trends follow the change in the surface dipole, and ignoring double-layer effects can introduce significant errors in computed energetics.

Ind. Eng. Chem. Res.2023First author

Techno-economic Analysis and Optimization of Intensified, Large-Scale Hydrogen Production with Membrane Reactors

D. M. Sweeney, V. Alves, S. Sakhai, S. Dinh, F. V. Lima

Steam methane reforming supplies most of the world's hydrogen but is energy intensive. I modeled a large-scale reforming plant intensified with palladium membrane reactors, then ran economic analyses and constrained nonlinear optimization over the full process. Even with higher compressor, membrane, and electricity costs, the membrane reactor design lowers natural gas use and total annual cost compared to the conventional plant.
